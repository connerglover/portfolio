1.1.6 is the digital counterpart to 1.1.4: identify parts by what they do rather than by what they look like. Eight problems, covering six combinational gates, a D flip-flop and a 555 clock. I built all eight circuits on a single Multisim sheet, driving every gate input from an SPDT switch between GND and +5 V and reading inputs and outputs on digital probes thresholded at 2.5 V against a 5.00 V supply. Binding all six X-switches to `Space` and all six Y-switches to `B` was a mistake I kept — it toggles every gate at once, which is fast for stepping through combinations but means the sheet never shows six independent states, and circuit 3's Y-switch started opposite the others and stayed out of phase for the whole run.

Problems 1–6 are the gates: 74LS04N inverter, 74LS08N AND, 74LS00N NAND, 74LS32N OR, 74LS02N NOR and 74LS86N XOR. Toggling all four input combinations on each and recording the output gave six truth tables, all of which matched simulation. The names turn out to be conditions on the inputs rather than tables to memorise — all inputs high for AND, at least one high for OR, exactly one high for XOR — with the N-prefixed parts being those same functions with the output inverted. Phrasing them as "all" and "at least one" instead of a fixed count is what makes them carry over to wider parts like a 3-input AND. OR and XOR agree on three of the four rows and split only when both inputs are high: XOR is OR with the both-high case excluded.

Problem 7 wires a 74LS74N with D fed back from NOT_Q, which makes it a toggle — each active edge loads the opposite of the current output. PR and CLR are active-low, so tying both to +5 V leaves the clock in sole control. Starting from CLK low and NOT_Q low, taking CLK from GND to 5 V turned Q off, returning it to GND did nothing at all, and the next GND-to-5 V transition turned it back on. The part is positive-edge-triggered, which I checked rather than assumed by holding CLK high for about three seconds and watching Q sit still. Q and NOT_Q were complementary in every state, and between edges Q held whatever it had regardless of D. That holding is the actual point of the part: one stored bit.

Problem 8 is three LM555CN astables, identical except for the timing capacitor — 12 µF, 6 µF and 24 µF, each with R_A = 100 Ω, R_B = 330 Ω and a 10 nF control-voltage bypass. Measuring at the OUT pin, taking the period as the mean interval between successive rising edges over every complete cycle and discarding the first to exclude the power-on transient, gave 6.344 ms and 157.6 Hz at 12 µF, 3.172 ms and 315.2 Hz at 6 µF, and 12.687 ms and 78.8 Hz at 24 µF, with cycle-to-cycle jitter under 0.5 µs everywhere. Against T = 0.693(R_A + 2R_B)C those predict 6.320 ms, 3.160 ms and 12.640 ms, so the measurements run +0.38%, +0.39% and +0.37% long — a positive bias of consistent size in all three, which is what ideal equations should do when they ignore the saturation voltage of the internal discharge transistor and the finite comparator switching time. Halving C doubled the frequency and doubling it halved the frequency, both to four figures, while the duty cycle stayed at 56.4% in every circuit: C multiplies t_high and t_low equally, so only the resistor ratio sets duty.

Three things I recorded instead of quietly correcting. R_A = 100 Ω is below sensible practice — the discharge transistor sinks roughly 50 mA through it each cycle, inside the 200 mA rating so the simulation runs, but at least 1 kΩ is the normal floor. The activity asks whether you can see the output blink, and at 78.8–315.2 Hz you cannot: that is far above the ~60 Hz flicker-fusion threshold, so all three probes read as steadily lit. The worksheet almost certainly intended 100 kΩ and 330 kΩ, which would give 6.32 s, 3.16 s and 12.64 s and a visible blink. And the scope probe was reversed on the 12 µF and 24 µF captures, so those traces swing −5 V to 0 V rather than 0 to +5 V — the display is inverted, but edge timing, period and duty cycle are not. I built the circuits with the printed values and measured what they actually did, and since both comparison answers depend only on ratios they hold either way.

Between them the three parts are the three kinds of thing the Random Number Generator at the end of this unit needs: combinational logic, whose output is fixed entirely by the present inputs; sequential logic, whose output depends on when the clock transitioned and persists between edges; and a clock source to drive the second from the first, with its period set by two resistors and a capacitor.
